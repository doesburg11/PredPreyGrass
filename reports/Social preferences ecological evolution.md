# Trace Social Preference Evolution Through Ecology

Yes—there is substantial related work, but it is distributed across neighboring literatures rather than concentrated in an exact precedent. The closest computational analogue is Huang et al.’s spatial wolf–caribou model, which evolves collective escape behavior with empathy; the clearest work on evolving preferences is Hetzer and Sornette’s public-goods model; and ecological studies separately connect social traits, movement, resources, and population dynamics. The saved project notes describe the target module as a frozen-policy experiment that assigns attraction or avoidance biases but does not yet inherit or select those preferences. Thus, the defensible research opportunity is not “social behavior in predator–prey systems,” which is established, but the explicit evolution of a separately represented social-preference trait within a continuous-space ecological multi-agent system. This is a promising candidate distinction, not a claim of priority: the search recorded in the notes was bounded and cannot establish that no closer work exists.

## Predator–prey studies already evolve social escape and movement

The closest joint match to spatial predator–prey ecology and social behavior is Fangwei Huang, Madoka Takahara, Ivan Tanev, and Katsunori Shimohara, “Emergence of Collective Escaping Strategies of Various Sized Teams of Empathic Caribou Agents in the Wolf-caribou Predator-prey Problem,” *IEEJ Transactions on Electronics, Information and Systems* 138(5):619–626 (2018). The study uses genetic programming to evolve escape strategies for caribou teams in a two-dimensional toroidal pursuit world; its abstract reports that incorporating empathy improves escape efficiency and effectiveness. This rules out claiming that evolved collective escape or empathy-inspired behavior in spatial predator–prey simulations is itself new. The important distinction is that the cited account evolves escape strategies with empathy incorporated, not an independently inherited empathy or attraction/avoidance parameter ([J-STAGE article](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ieejeiss/138/5/138_619/_article/-char/en); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1541/ieejeiss.138.619)).

Other studies fill in adjacent ecological mechanisms. Christoph Netz, Hanno Hildenbrandt, and Franz J. Weissing, “Complex eco-evolutionary dynamics induced by the coevolution of predator–prey movement strategies,” *Evolutionary Ecology* 36(1):1–17 (2021), model heritable movement strategies in predators and prey on a resource landscape, linking behavioral coevolution to changing spatial patterns ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10682-021-10140-x)). Randal S. Olson, Arend Hintze, Fred C. Dyer, Jason H. Moore, and Christoph Adami, “Exploring the coevolution of predator and prey morphology and behavior,” in *Proceedings of the 2016 Conference on Artificial Life* (2016), report a coevolutionary cycle involving prey swarming and predator visual systems ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.7551/978-0-262-33936-0-ch045)). Zoltán Tóth and Gabriella Kőmüves, “Social information-mediated population dynamics in non-grouping prey,” *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology* 76, article 87 (2022), model predator-avoidance information spreading among prey and its effects on equilibrium abundance ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-022-03215-4)). These are strong precedents for ecological feedback and social effects, but they do not establish the target combination of an explicitly evolving preference trait and learned multi-agent policies.

## Preference evolution and ecological feedback supply complementary foundations

The clearest direct precedent for evolving other-regarding preferences is Gerd S. Hetzer and Didier Sornette, “An Evolutionary Model of Cooperation, Fairness and Altruistic Punishment in Public Good Games,” *PLOS ONE* 8(11):e77041 (2013). Their model combines expected-utility preferences with evolutionary dynamics in a public-goods game, selecting among agents with different fairness-related traits. It is the closest cited source for treating preference itself—not just a behavior produced by a policy—as an object of evolutionary change, although its well-mixed game setting is unlike a predator–prey ecosystem ([article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0077041); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0077041)). For a broader framing, David Alger, “Evolutionarily stable preferences,” *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 378:20210505 (2023), reviews models in which inherited preferences influence choices and the resulting fitness differences feed back into selection ([article and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2021.0505)).

Ecology-focused bridges include Jean-François Le Galliard, Raphaël Ferrière, and Ulrich Dieckmann, “Adaptive Evolution of Social Traits: Origin, Trajectories, and Correlations of Altruism and Mobility,” *The American Naturalist* (2005), which jointly considers altruism, mobility, costs, and habitat saturation ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/427090)); and Mark B. Bonsall and Alex J. Wright, “Altruism and the evolution of resource generalism and specialism,” *Ecology and Evolution* (2012), which couples resource–consumer dynamics to evolutionary invasion and resource-use strategies ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.206)). These support asking how social traits and ecological conditions co-shape each other, even though neither implements the target’s spatial action bias.

The underlying concepts should not be collapsed into one “sociality” variable. W. D. Hamilton’s “The genetical evolution of social behaviour I,” *Journal of Theoretical Biology* 7(1):1–16 (1964), grounds the evolution of costly social behavior in relatedness and inclusive fitness ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/2281218a0)). Robert L. Trivers’s “The Evolution of Reciprocal Altruism,” *The Quarterly Review of Biology* 46(1):35–57 (1971), concerns contingent help when individuals interact repeatedly ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/406755)). Ernst Fehr and Klaus M. Schmidt’s “A Theory of Fairness, Competition, and Cooperation,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 114(3):817–868 (1999), parameterizes aversion to unequal payoffs ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1162/003355399556151)); Paul A. M. Van Lange’s “The Pursuit of Joint Outcomes and Equality in Outcomes: An Integrative Model of Social Value Orientation,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 77(2):337–349 (1999), describes preferences over own, joint, and relative outcomes ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.77.2.337)). A spatial attraction/avoidance bias is not automatically altruism, reciprocity, inequity aversion, or SVO: it changes movement toward or away from conspecifics, without necessarily valuing their welfare or returning help.

## The potential contribution lies in separating preference from policy

The saved notes describe the target module’s current design as applying a per-predator attraction/avoidance bias to logits from a frozen PPO policy. Preference values are externally assigned for an individual’s lifetime, not inherited; training, reward, body-trait inheritance, and physics are not changed. The noted mixed-preference experiment did not find a clear population-level proximity improvement over neutral. On that evidence, the current project is a useful behavioral-intervention or interaction-pattern experiment, but it is not yet a test of selection on social preferences ([module README](../predpreygrass/evolutionary/continuous_space/eco_evolutionary_social_preference/README.md); [mixed-preference results](../predpreygrass/evolutionary/continuous_space/eco_evolutionary_social_preference/MIXED_PREFERENCES_RESULTS.md)).

The most defensible candidate novelty would emerge if the preference became an explicit transmissible trait with variation, and reproductive or survival differences in the ecological simulation changed its frequency across generations. That would join pieces found separately in evolved social escape, ecological movement coevolution, preference-selection theory, and multi-agent learning. It would also clarify mechanism: attraction might increase safety through aggregation, intensify competition for grass, or expose individuals to different predator risks, so its fitness effect need not have one sign. Maurício Cantor et al., “The importance of individual-to-society feedbacks in animal ecology and evolution,” *Journal of Animal Ecology* 90(1):27–44 (2021), provides a useful conceptual frame for this loop from individual social decisions to group structure, exposure, and selection ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2656.13336)).

For the learning-and-environment side, Michael Kölle et al., “Aquarium: A Comprehensive Framework for Exploring Predator-Prey Dynamics Through Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning Algorithms,” *Proceedings of the 16th International Conference on Agents and Artificial Intelligence*, vol. 1, pp. 59–70 (2024), describe a continuous-space predator–prey MARL environment and PPO demonstrations. It is a useful environment and methods comparator, not evidence that preference evolution has already been solved ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.5220/0012382300003636); [implementation](https://github.com/michaelkoelle/marl-aquarium)). Huang’s 2019 dissertation, *Evolving social behavior of caribou agents in wolf-caribou predator-prey pursuit problem*, is a secondary source for the caribou work’s methodological history ([repository](https://doshisha.repo.nii.ac.jp/records/1533); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.14988/di.2019.0000000577)). A 2025 adjacent lead is Yang, Zhao, and Wang, “Evolution of cooperation in spatial public goods games driven by reinforcement learning and environmental feedback,” *Chaos, Solitons & Fractals* 199:116592 (2025); the saved notes verified its bibliographic title and DOI but not its abstract or detailed results, so it should be checked before being used for specific claims ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chaos.2025.116592)).

## Position the next study around a testable selection question

The cleanest framing is: **Does selection on an inherited conspecific-attraction/avoidance trait produce repeatable behavioral and ecological outcomes in a spatial predator–prey–resource system?** Keep preference, learned policy, and behavior analytically distinct. A useful staged comparison would hold policies fixed while preferences are assigned, then allow preference inheritance and selection with policy held fixed, and only afterward test joint policy/preference adaptation. Track trait distributions and lineage-level reproduction alongside proximity or alignment, predation risk, survival, resource access, and population persistence; use independent evolutionary replicates rather than treating encounters within one run as independent evidence.

For additional searches, combine terms for each axis rather than relying on “social preference” alone: “evolved social preferences spatial predator prey,” “heritable sociality attraction avoidance movement evolution,” “empathy collective escape genetic programming caribou,” “eco-evolutionary social traits predator prey movement,” “social information predator avoidance population dynamics,” and “reinforcement learning spatial public goods environmental feedback.” Also search preference under alternate labels such as prosociality, altruism, cooperation, aggregation, schooling, social information, and movement strategy. The saved search notes consulted scholarly metadata and abstracts, DOI and publisher pages, and repository information, but they flag limited index coverage and terminology variation; **“no exact match found in this search” is justified, whereas “first ever” is not**.

## Conclusion

The literature gives the project a strong lineage, not a one-to-one template: Huang et al. is closest in social behavior and predator–prey space, Hetzer and Sornette in selection on preference, and Le Galliard et al. and Bonsall and Wright in ecological feedback. The differentiating question is whether a separately defined social preference—not merely a social behavior or learned policy—can be inherited and selected through ecological fitness in this setting. That distinction makes the next research step concrete while keeping any novelty claim appropriately provisional.
