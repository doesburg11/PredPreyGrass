# Social preference evolution: evidence notes

## Does meaningful related work exist, and what is closest to the project?

### Takeaway

Yes. There is substantial foundational work on the evolution of altruistic and reciprocal behavior, theoretical work that explicitly allows preferences to evolve, and ecological models linking social traits to population/resource dynamics. The closest conceptual matches are not exact matches: the literature located does not verify a study combining continuously spatial predator–prey–resource ecology, learned multi-agent policies, and inherited individual social-preference parameters in the same model.

### Cited Findings

- **Current project scope (repository documentation, checked 2026-10-27):** the module is described as a fixed-policy proof of concept. It adds a per-predator attraction/avoidance bias to logits from a frozen PPO policy; preferences are assigned externally for an individual's lifetime and are explicitly *not* inherited. The README says rewards, PPO training, body-trait inheritance, and physics are not changed. Its later mixed-preference experiment compares uniform and mixed conditions and reports that the tested mixed population did not show a clear population-level proximity improvement over neutral. This makes it an experiment about behavioral interventions and interaction patterns, not yet selection on social preferences. [Project README](https://github.com/doesburg11/PredPreyGrass-archive/blob/main/eco_evolutionary_social_preference/README.md); [mixed-preference results](https://github.com/doesburg11/PredPreyGrass-archive/blob/main/eco_evolutionary_social_preference/MIXED_PREFERENCES_RESULTS.md)
- **Closest explicit preference-evolution model:** Hetzer and Sornette (2013), “An Evolutionary Model of Cooperation, Fairness and Altruistic Punishment in Public Good Games,” *PLOS ONE* 8(11):e77041, published 2013-11-19. They combine an expected-utility model with evolutionary population dynamics in a voluntary-contribution/public-goods game. Agents' traits determine contribution and punishment behavior; fitness-weighted transmission and selection act on traits. They report two regimes—defection or coordination—depending on self- versus other-regarding preferences, and identify disadvantageous inequity aversion as evolutionarily dominant/stable in a heterogeneous population initially containing self-regarding agents. It is a close match for explicitly evolving fairness-related preferences, but its setting is a well-mixed human public-goods interaction, not ecological resource competition, spatial movement, or learned PPO control. [Peer-reviewed open-access article and abstract](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0077041) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0077041)
- **Closest interaction-learning / animal-cooperation model:** Leimar and Bshary (2024), “Social bond dynamics and the evolution of helping,” *PNAS* 121, published 2024-03-07. In a game-theoretic model inspired by food sharing in vampire bats, repeated help builds relationship bonds using an association-learning process analogous to the Rescorla–Wagner rule. Bonds affect partner choice and help amounts; reciprocity in bonded pairs can evolve toward looser or stricter forms. The authors find that small social neighborhoods support evolutionary stability of helping, help costs need to be low relative to benefits, and the evolved reciprocity is not necessarily immediate or strict. The model also produces evolved preferences to form bonds within, and avoid bonds across, temporary group boundaries. This directly informs interaction-dependent learning, memory, partner choice, and sociality, but the evolving object is helping/relationship strategy rather than a general inherited utility preference, and it is not predator–prey ecology. [DOI and article](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2317736121)
- **Closest eco-evolutionary treatment of altruism and ecology:** Le Galliard, Ferrière, and Dieckmann (2005), “Adaptive Evolution of Social Traits: Origin, Trajectories, and Correlations of Altruism and Mobility,” *The American Naturalist*. Their adaptive-evolution analysis jointly evolves altruism and mobility and studies how physiological costs and eco-evolutionary feedbacks shape trajectories. Habitat saturation—around residents and unoccupied sites—is a key mediator; the order in which altruism and mobility evolve depends on their costs. This is a strong ecological analogue for social-trait/environment feedback and movement, but does not model payoff inequity, SVO, or a learned action policy. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/427090)
- **Resource-consumer ecological model:** Bonsall and Wright (2012), “Altruism and the evolution of resource generalism and specialism,” *Ecology and Evolution*. Using an evolutionary-invasion approach coupled to resource–consumer dynamics, they study when altruistic cooperation allows resource specialization to arise and persist, including differences between complementary and substitutable resources. Their results connect the strength of resource preferences and altruism to generalism/specialism. This is unusually relevant to ecological feedbacks and resource use, but altruism is a modeled behavior/trait rather than the project's spatial action-logit preference. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.206)
- **Current synthesis of preference evolution:** Alger (2023), “Evolutionarily stable preferences,” *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 378:20210505, published 2023-03-19. This review describes “indirect” evolutionary models where preferences are inherited, preferences determine strategy choices, and those strategies determine evolutionary success, bringing proximate choice and ultimate selection into one framework. It is the most useful high-level source for designing the missing preference-inheritance layer, but is a synthesis rather than a predator–prey application. [DOI/article](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2021.0505)
- **Recent adjacent pointer:** Yang, Zhao, and Wang (2025), “Evolution of cooperation in spatial public goods games driven by reinforcement learning and environmental feedback,” *Chaos, Solitons & Fractals* 199:116592 (Crossref lists publication month October 2025). The title and DOI establish an adjacent spatial-game/RL/environment-feedback line. I could not retrieve its abstract or verify detailed model/results in this pass, so treat it as a lead to inspect rather than evidence for a specific finding or a direct match. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chaos.2025.116592)

### Inferences

- The key modeling distinction is whether the inherited entity is a **preference parameter** (which shapes decisions), a **behavioral strategy** (for example, cooperate or punish), a **learned relationship state** (such as a bond), or a **policy parameter**. The cited papers instantiate different levels; comparing them as if they all evolve “preferences” would be misleading.
- For this project, the most useful combined precedent is Hetzer & Sornette for preference/trait selection, Le Galliard et al. and Bonsall & Wright for feedback with ecology, and Leimar & Bshary for learning and interaction structure.

### Gaps

- A bounded search found no verified direct match for the project's complete combination: continuous spatial ecology with predator, prey, and grass; multi-agent learned policies; an individual social-preference phenotype; and ecological fitness-based inheritance/selection. This is a scoped finding, not proof that no such work exists.
- The 2025 RL/spatial-public-goods paper is only title-level evidence here; its abstract and results need separate verification before detailed comparison.

## What do altruism, reciprocity, inequity aversion, and social value orientation add?

### Takeaway

These are related but non-equivalent constructs. The project currently implements a local directional attraction/avoidance bias, whereas reciprocity requires contingent responses over interactions, inequity aversion values relative payoff distributions, and social value orientation (SVO) concerns weights on own versus others' outcomes.

### Cited Findings

- **Altruism and kin selection:** Hamilton's 1964 “The genetical evolution of social behaviour I” formalizes how a behavior that reduces actor fitness can spread when it benefits relatives sufficiently (the inclusive-fitness/kin-selection foundation). This is a theory of selection on social behavior, not a claim that organisms represent an explicit altruistic utility. [Nature article/DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/2281218a0)
- **Direct reciprocity:** Trivers (1971), “The Evolution of Reciprocal Altruism,” *Quarterly Review of Biology* 46(1):35–57, explains how costly help can be favored between individuals likely to meet again and respond contingently. This motivates memory, repeated encounters, and identity/partner-specific history—features absent from a momentary nearest-neighbor directional bias. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1086/406755)
- **Reciprocity as strategy evolution:** Axelrod and Hamilton (1981), “The Evolution of Cooperation,” *Science* 211:1390–1396, studied repeated Prisoner's Dilemma strategies and showed how reciprocity can sustain cooperation under suitable repeated-interaction conditions. It evolves strategies in a game-theoretic population, not preference weights or ecological agents. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.7466396)
- **Inequity aversion utility:** Fehr and Schmidt (1999), “A Theory of Fairness, Competition, and Cooperation,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 114(3):817–868, model utility as own material payoff minus weighted disadvantageous and advantageous payoff differences relative to others. They use this utility to account for behavior in bargaining, market, and cooperation settings. The paper supplies a parameterization for inequity aversion; it does not itself evolve those coefficients in an ecological population. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1162/003355399556151)
- **Alternative fairness model:** Bolton and Ockenfels (2000), “ERC: A Theory of Equity, Reciprocity, and Competition,” *American Economic Review* 90(1):166–193, model utility as depending on own payoff and relative share of total payoff. It is a conceptual alternative for distinguishing aversion to unequal outcomes from other-regarding concern, not a social-evolution/ecology model. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.90.1.166)
- **SVO construct:** Van Lange (1999), “The Pursuit of Joint Outcomes and Equality in Outcomes: An Integrative Model of Social Value Orientation,” *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 77(2):337–349, organizes prosocial, individualistic, and competitive orientations by how people value joint outcomes, own outcomes, and relative equality. This is a psychological/decision construct, not an evolutionary dynamics paper. It could motivate a parameterization of action values or rewards, but it should not be equated with attraction to nearby conspecifics. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.77.2.337)
- **Preference evolution bridge:** Alger and Weibull (2019), “Evolutionary Models of Preference Formation,” *Annual Review of Economics* 11(1):329–354, reviews models in which material fitness selects among preferences, rather than holding preference distributions fixed. It is a useful conceptual bridge from economic social-preference models to inherited social traits. [DOI/article](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-080218-030255)

### Inferences

- If the research question is “do attraction/avoidance tendencies evolve?”, encode that tendency as a transmissible individual trait and measure fitness consequences. If it is “does fairness evolve?”, the preference should depend on others' material outcomes, not simply distance or action alignment.
- A robust design could keep distinct heritable dimensions (e.g., spatial attraction, own/other payoff weights, advantageous/disadvantageous inequity coefficients, and reciprocity sensitivity) rather than collapse them into a single “sociality” scalar.
- The action-logit bias currently measures an intervention on the frozen policy's movement choice. It does not by itself establish altruism (a cost to self for another's benefit), reciprocity (contingent returned help), or prosocial SVO (weight on joint outcomes).

### Gaps

- The project has no current measure of payoff inequality, costly help, reciprocal exchange, partner-specific memory, or own-versus-other utility. Those are needed to make direct construct-level comparisons with inequity aversion, reciprocity, or SVO.
- A policy bias used during PPO training would need mathematically consistent action probabilities/log-probabilities in the learning objective; the project README itself cautions against using its post-inference sampler for PPO without that correction. [Project README](https://github.com/doesburg11/PredPreyGrass-archive/blob/main/eco_evolutionary_social_preference/README.md)

## What is the literature gap and the most useful comparison?

### Takeaway

There is a solid body of related theory, including some direct models of evolved fairness preferences, but no verified one-to-one precedent for this project. A careful paper/project comparison should position the module as an experimental bridge from fixed-policy social bias toward eco-evolutionary preference selection.

### Cited Findings

- The preference-evolution literature explicitly combines inherited preferences, preference-dependent choices, and selection on resulting outcomes; Alger's review identifies this as a route to connect proximate behavioral mechanisms with evolutionary explanations. [Alger 2023](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2021.0505)
- Ecological social-trait models show that movement and social behavior can coevolve through local population structure, and that resource-consumer feedback can alter the conditions for altruism and specialization. [Le Galliard et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1086/427090); [Bonsall & Wright 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.206)
- Interaction learning can affect evolutionary outcomes without being identical to preference inheritance: Leimar and Bshary's 2024 model lets helping exchanges build bonds, which then change partner choice and assistance; both helping and bond-related tendencies have evolutionary consequences. [Leimar & Bshary 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2317736121)
- Explicitly evolved fairness-related preferences have been modeled in public-goods interactions, but the mechanism differs from the project's movement bias and the ecological setting differs from its predator–prey–grass system. [Hetzer & Sornette 2013](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0077041)

### Inferences

- A direct next comparison would ask whether the same ecological environment produces differential reproductive success among heritable attraction, neutral, and avoidance phenotypes, while separately measuring behavior (approach/alignment), social exposure, survival, offspring, and population/resource outcomes. This turns the present “does the bias change behavior?” test into the evolutionary question “which preference types persist or spread, and under what ecological conditions?”
- For an interaction-learning arm, compare inherited fixed preference against within-lifetime learning from repeated partner encounters; report these as distinct mechanisms. Partner identity and encounter history are central to reciprocal helping and learned bonds, while the existing bias targets the nearest visible conspecific.
- Suitable negative/neutral controls include neutral preference, no visible partner, and a matched simulation where preference does not affect fitness or reproduction. Sufficient independent population-level replicates are needed; episodes or encounter-level samples are not independent evolutionary replicates.

### Gaps

- The 2024 Leimar–Bshary model is about helping in stable animal groups, not spatial predator–prey dynamics. The 2012 Bonsall–Wright model includes resource-consumer ecology but not the project's continuous movement and learned multi-agent policies. The 2013 Hetzer–Sornette model explicitly evolves fairness-related preferences but in public-goods games. These are near matches along separate axes, not direct matches.
- This note is a targeted evidence map, not a systematic review. Searches favored stable DOI/publisher sources and abstracts; terminology differs across evolutionary biology, economics, social psychology, and multi-agent learning, so additional papers may be indexed under “other-regarding preferences,” “sociality,” “cooperation,” “cultural evolution,” or “social learning” rather than “social preference.”
